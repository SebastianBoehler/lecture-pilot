# LecturePilot learning evidence audit

25 September 2026. Scope: static review of the current checkout against the 25 locally archived full-text PDFs, the [paper-level reading notes](2026-09-25-paper-content-and-access-notes.md), and earlier [neuroscience](2026-09-06-neuroscience-learning-evidence.md), [media](2026-09-06-display-media-learning-evidence.md), and [AI](2026-09-06-ai-learning-and-evaluation-update.md) notes. I revisited methods, results and limitations for the decisions below; this was not a new systematic search or a line-by-line replication of every analysis. The archive contains author manuscripts and two dissertations as well as published PDFs. This audit is about _fit between mechanisms and implementation_, not measured LecturePilot efficacy. No learner study, live browser run, assessment calibration or code test was performed for this report.

Implementation update, 25 September 2026: the learner dashboard now lists unfinished reviews due in the next seven days from the authenticated, revision-bound review queue. The audit below describes the state before that change; assessment calibration, grouped checkpoints and calendar delivery remain proposals.

## Executive judgment

LecturePilot has an unusually explicit **learning-evidence architecture**: professor-approved source-backed outcomes, a baseline attempt, selected support, a fresh unaided exit, and a delayed changed-form task. That fits the strongest relevant learning research better than optimizing for assisted exercise completion. Its exact revision bindings and honest event categories also make a future evaluation possible. These are implementation strengths, **not evidence that LecturePilot outperforms instruction or another tutor**. The repository's own [evaluation contract](../evaluation-contract.md) says the current checks used synthetic local data and no efficacy study.

The highest-value gaps are the _delivery and validity_ of this architecture: later reviews are scheduled but not shown before they become due; the app has no learner study calendar; AI judgments and generated task equivalence have not been calibrated against independent human scoring; and the answer/feedback flow is still one item at a time with separate optional multiple-choice quizzes. A [same-day synthetic checkpoint benchmark](2026-09-25-gate-assessment-benchmark.md) now provides an initial model regression signal, but does not close the human-calibration gap. A science-grounded product experiment should focus on **completed delayed independent transfer**, not clicks, quiz volume, goal completion, or polished visuals.

## Research comparison: supported, uncertain, or mismatched

1. **Retrieval and spacing — strong match, incomplete follow-through.** [Adesope et al.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306) and the classroom [Agarwal et al. review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9) support retrieval opportunities across settings, with heterogeneity. [Cepeda et al.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) found that the useful spacing interval depends on the eventual retention interval. LecturePilot already asks for independent and delayed attempts. The gap is making scheduled reviews visible, reachable, and recoverable after missed dates. There is no basis here for a universal two-day or seven-day rule. [Pan and Rickard](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000124) also show that a retrieval benefit need not transfer to a different response form: a delayed _changed_ task is a better test of the product promise than repeating the same item.

2. **AI tutoring — well-chosen guardrails, effect unknown.** In [Bastani et al.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), unrestricted AI improved assisted math practice yet harmed immediate unaided performance; a hint-first version largely removed the harm without proving a gain. LecturePilot's attempt-first/help/fresh-exit contract responds directly. [Tutor CoPilot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017) and [LearnLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633) study AI with human tutors in the loop, on different populations/outcomes; they do not validate an autonomous university tutor. Professor authoring approval is valuable governance, but the moment-to-moment tutor is still an empirical question. Logically, a provider can return plausible but incorrect evidence IDs even when the server owns the next transition.

3. **Feedback and worked examples — directionally grounded, quality is decisive.** [Atkinson, Renkl and Merrill](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774) found transfer benefits from faded examples plus principle-focused self-explanation in structured tasks. Tübingen [Wagner et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2023.101844) found that brief corrective feedback did not reliably add to prior instruction and could hurt far transfer, while elaborated actionable feedback could help. The existing approved hint ladder and evidence-specific response design are a good platform. Measure whether real feedback names the missing reasoning and gives the next _doable_ step without disclosing the hidden exit. Do not force a worked example or explanation after every answer.

4. **Self-regulated learning — partial match.** [Panadero](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00422) describes planning, monitoring/control and appraisal across SRL models, not a proven feature recipe. In a five-week digital higher-education experiment, [Martins van Jaarsveld et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2025.105441) found better self-reported goal attainment from monitoring prompts but no significant grade difference. The product has a session goal and a progress flow, but limited forward planning and no simple “planned reviews vs actually completed” view. A calendar can deliver a plan; calendar connection itself has no demonstrated learning effect in these papers.

5. **Media/interface — principled selection beats media quantity.** [Berney and Bétrancourt](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.06.005) found a small average advantage for animation over static graphics with high heterogeneity; [Ploetzner et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w) found animations helped a motion-identification task while a still supported spatial-layout identification. A [course-map study](https://doi.org/10.3991/ijet.v10i7.4620) increased optional task completion without a significant posttest grade difference. LecturePilot's restrained text-first canvas, outline and optional media are defensible. Add a figure only for the relation or motion it can actually explain, and test a matching explanation/application question. Do not infer that visual neatness or task completion proves learning.

6. **Neuroscience — mechanisms, not feature mandates.** [Retrieval fMRI](https://doi.org/10.1002/brb3.1909), [spacing/re-encoding](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115232), [sleep/relearning](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616659930) and [sleep-related theta](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003938) make a plausible mechanistic case for later remembering after retrieval and intervals. They do not establish a personalized scheduling algorithm, a brain-based dashboard, or that an app should monitor sleep. Preserve gaps between study and review, make resumption easy, and judge success with behavior. The Tübingen [Russ study](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000971) is a direct caution about feature stacking: in a 344-student physics classroom study, generation, retrieval and their combination had no significant overall conceptual-knowledge benefit at immediate or eight-week tests; quality-dependent findings were exploratory.

### How a grouped checkpoint should work

The user's proposed “one multiple-choice, then one open answer, maybe three pages” is a useful **assessment design option**. Treat it as one approved learning target with separately identified items and rubric coverage, rather than a cosmetic carousel. An example flow:

1. **Item 1: discriminate.** Choose between plausible models/steps; save the answer and, where informative, a one-sentence reason. The choice is diagnostic, never sufficient alone for mastery.
2. **Item 2: explain or apply.** Use a fresh scenario and ask for the reasoning that the criterion requires. This is the stronger independent evidence. Do not reveal the item-1 answer or a worked solution before item 2 if it would disclose the reasoning being tested.
3. **Feedback and continuation.** After the sequence, give criterion-specific actionable feedback. If help was used, mark that sequence supported and issue an unseen approved equivalent task for the unaided exit. Keep the later delayed task separate and unchanged.

Persist per-item answers, attempts, exposure and revision under one gate; derive gate completion from **required evidence**, not “two of three correct.” Preserve professor review of exact items, answer keys, distractors, source anchors and comparable difficulty. Pagination should let learners revisit their own earlier reasoning and any givens needed for the current item without exposing answers; hiding essential context can add memory load, a risk discussed by Atkinson et al. Keep the focused mode's hidden materials and reload behavior. The existing optional quiz cannot simply be counted as a gate item: [`CanvasQuizBlock.tsx`](../../apps/web/src/CanvasQuizBlock.tsx) records choice outcomes separately, whereas the gate state machine in [`coaching_transitions.py`](../../apps/api/src/lecturepilot/coaching_transitions.py) handles one approved task per stage. This is a cross-stack change to practice design, publication validation, learner state, assessment and analytics. A first prototype should be one goal and two items, with a reviewed single-item comparator.

### What a learner calendar should mean

A schedule should be generated from _real pending reviews_ and learner availability, not from lecture dates alone. Show “next review,” “overdue,” and “completed” with a bounded weekly load. Keep any exam-date planning distinct from the professor's lecture unlock dates. Offer a lightweight reminder that opens the exact current review, and retract/update an event when the review is completed or its gate revision changes. Avoid estimating “mastery” from scheduling or attendance. If Google Calendar sync is built, use narrow OAuth scopes, opt-in per learner and a clear disconnect path; no course PDFs or learner answers in events. This is an engineering recommendation, not a claim about a validated calendar intervention.

## What needs evidence before stronger claims

- **No causal product effect yet:** approved design and a plausible mechanism are necessary but insufficient. Run a consented, preregistered comparison on matched content with a delayed, blinded changed-task outcome, common follow-up window and attrition reporting. The existing [evaluation contract](../evaluation-contract.md) gives the starting outcome definitions.
- **Assessment validity:** check false passes/false failures, score drift across subjects, equivalent difficulty across hidden variants and language versions, and feedback leakage. Human-scored samples are needed before “mastery” or “transfer” claims.
- **Calendar and grouped questions:** test each separately first, at similar total study time, then study interactions. More questions and more scheduled events can raise burden; completion and self-reported goal attainment are secondary process outcomes.
- **Neuroscience and visual design:** no fMRI activation, sleep result, screenshot rating or animation average can substitute for a delayed learning test of this exact interface.
