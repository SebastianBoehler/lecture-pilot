# Neuroscience and learning through a display

Research checked 2026-09-06 for the presentation freeze. This is a targeted
evidence review, not a systematic review or an efficacy evaluation of LecturePilot.
Primary papers were found through journal, PubMed/PMC and author-repository
searches. Methods, outcomes and limitations were inspected in the linked full
texts; the preliminary AI-writing study below received only a targeted scope check.

## What the neuroscience can support

Retrieving an answer is another learning event: it can engage memory binding and
semantic processing, not merely read an unchanged memory. Spacing changes the
opportunity to recover and re-encode earlier experience. These mechanisms are
compatible with LecturePilot's independent attempts and later checks; they do not
prove that its task selection, assessment or interface produces durable learning.
See [Wing et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2013.04.004) and
[Zou et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115232).

A brain-region activation, connectivity estimate or pupil response is a measured
signal with several possible explanations. It is not a mastery score. Educational
claims need behavioral outcomes on the intended knowledge and transfer task,
preferably after a delay. Below, confidence describes the bounded finding, not
confidence that adding the proposed feature will improve LecturePilot.

## Ten original peer-reviewed papers

### 1. Retrieval can strengthen later memory

Wing, Marsh & Cabeza (2013), _Neuropsychologia_,
[Neural correlates of retrieval-based memory enhancement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2013.04.004).
[Author full text](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c8baca1e5f7d136349ea789/t/5e73a600d1307f4358ca58af/1584637443247/1-s2.0-S002839321300119X-main.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** 22 analyzed college-aged adults; counterbalanced word-pair
  retrieval versus restudy during fMRI; cued recall 24 hours later.
- **Finding:** Retrieved items were remembered better. Hippocampal and temporal
  activity, and hippocampal connectivity, were associated with later success.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate mechanistic evidence; small sample, associative
  recall, no course-level conceptual-transfer test. Imaging is correlational.
- **App inference:** Preserve an attempt before showing the answer. Measure the
  learner's later response separately from the explanation they just saw.

### 2. Spacing benefits depend on recovering the earlier experience

Zou, Kuhl, DuBrow & Hutchinson (online 2025-01-23; issue 2025-02-25), _Cell Reports_,
[Benefits of spaced learning are predicted by re-encoding](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115232).
[Author full text](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.uoregon.edu/dist/7/16268/files/2025/10/Zou-et-al.-2025-Benefits-of-spaced-learning-are-predicted-by-the-r.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** Eight adults aged 19–32, 30–40 7T sessions each over ten
  months; thousands of scene images; recognition and representational similarity.
- **Finding:** Spacing-related benefits tracked ventromedial prefrontal
  re-encoding and were strongest when the second encounter was recognized.
- **Confidence/limit:** Rich within-person evidence; only eight people and image
  recognition. It does not identify an optimal schedule for learning mathematics.
- **App inference:** Make later review reachable and failed retrieval repairable;
  avoid portraying one fixed delay as a neuroscience-derived optimum.

### 3. Sleep between study and relearning can support retention

Mazza et al. (2016-08-16), _Psychological Science_,
[Relearn faster and retain longer](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616659930).
[Full text](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/2016_FFC/Learn%20then%20sleep.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** Forty adults aged 18–29 randomized to daytime or overnight
  12-hour intervals; 16 Swahili–French pairs; relearning, one-week and six-month
  recall. A later control sample contained 20 participants.
- **Finding:** The overnight group required fewer relearning trials and retained
  more. Both main groups reached the same immediate relearning criterion.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate, narrow behavioral evidence; sleep opportunity,
  time of day and waking interference are intertwined. Neural replay was not measured.
- **App inference:** Keep completion distinct from later retention; encourage
  returning after a break without claiming to measure consolidation or sleep.

### 4. Curiosity about the target can accompany better memory

Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014-10), _Neuron_,
[States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/).

- **Design/outcome:** Adult volunteers; small fMRI sample with 19 face-memory
  analyses, plus a separate next-day behavioral experiment. Participants rated
  trivia curiosity; incidental faces appeared while awaiting answers.
- **Finding:** Higher curiosity accompanied better trivia recall and some
  incidental-face recognition; anticipatory neural signals predicted memory.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate for this paradigm. Curiosity was self-rated;
  dopamine release was not directly measured. Incidental benefits were small and
  depended on the recognition measure.
- **App inference:** Start from a meaningful uncertainty about the actual concept.
  This does not justify rewards, streaks or unrelated entertainment.

### 5. Curiosity can also interfere with nearby learning

Keller et al. (2024-03-18), _npj Science of Learning_,
[States of epistemic curiosity interfere with memory for incidental scholastic facts](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00234-w).

- **Design/outcome:** 239 analyzed US online adults aged 18–50; three timing
  variants; unrelated scholastic facts appeared around trivia; immediate
  multiple-choice memory test.
- **Finding:** Facts accompanying high-curiosity trivia were remembered worse
  across all three timings.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate boundary evidence. Online setting, quasi-random
  task-version assignment, no delayed test, and prior knowledge was not controlled.
  This changes material and outcome relative to the face-memory studies.
- **App inference:** A hook should explain why the target concept matters. Avoid
  sandwiching demanding material between unrelated exciting facts or rewards.

### 6. Explicit prediction selectively helps unexpected outcomes

Brod et al. (2022), _Psychonomic Bulletin & Review_,
[Explicitly predicting outcomes enhances learning of expectancy-violating information](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02124-x).
[Author full text](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/SUK/Brod_2022.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** 23 analyzed pupils aged 10–14 and 28 university students;
  numerical facts, immediate recall and pupillometry. Only the first experiment
  directly compared prediction with postdiction.
- **Finding:** Explicit prediction changed the relationship between surprise and
  memory, benefiting highly unexpected outcomes. The first experiment had no
  overall condition effect in its initial model and no pupil–memory association.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate, selective evidence; small samples, factual recall.
- **App inference:** For an appropriate teaching example, ask for a prediction,
  reveal a verified outcome, then explain the discrepancy. Do not promise a universal gain.

### 7. Physical experience can contribute to concept reasoning

Kontra, Lyons, Fischer & Beilock (2015-04-24), _Psychological Science_,
[Physical experience enhances science learning](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615569355).
[Author full text](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/voices.uchicago.edu/dist/8/1250/files/2018/07/Kontra-et-al-2015-Physical-experience-enhances-science-learning-239arn1.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** Three laboratory experiments plus randomized action versus
  observation in college physics. The fMRI study scanned 33 people; 19 completed
  its quiz. The field analysis retained 57 quiz and 59 homework participants.
- **Finding:** Manipulating spinning wheels improved angular-momentum reasoning;
  sensorimotor activation correlated with performance. Effects differed by problem type.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate, concept-specific evidence; substantial field
  exclusions. Statistical mediation does not establish neural necessity.
- **App inference:** Map interaction to the represented relationship. Actual force
  experience is not interchangeable with dragging a slider or wearing VR.

### 8. Unrelated laptop tasks can disrupt comprehension

Sana, Weston & Cepeda (2013), _Computers & Education_,
[Laptop multitasking hinders classroom learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.10.003).
[Full text](https://user.it.uu.se/~hesc0353/introIT/2012-Sana-Weston-Cepeda.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** Two simulated-lecture experiments, with 40 and 38 analyzed
  undergraduates; randomized multitasking or exposure to distracting peers;
  immediate factual and application questions.
- **Finding:** Multitasking participants scored 55% versus 66%; those viewing
  distracting peers scored 56% versus 73%.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate causal evidence for these distractions; attention
  was not directly measured. Neither a general laptop-versus-paper comparison nor
  a delayed-retention study.
- **App inference:** Avoid concurrent unrelated streams and unnecessary interface
  motion. A relevant diagram alongside its explanation is a different intervention.

### 9. Access to explanations can inflate perceived knowledge

Fisher, Goddu & Keil (2015), _Journal of Experimental Psychology: General_,
[Searching for explanations](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000070).
[Author full text](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/campuspress.yale.edu/dist/c/259/files/2020/05/fisher2015.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** Nine experiments with US online adults; search versus
  comparison conditions; self-assessed explanatory knowledge. Experiment 1a
  analyzed 197 of 202 participants; later experiments controlled content and time.
- **Finding:** Searching increased estimates of internal knowledge, including for
  unrelated questions; some boundary conditions removed the effect.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate for metacognitive judgments. This is not evidence
  of brain deterioration, poorer actual knowledge or the effects of a modern AI tutor.
- **App inference:** Preserve supported/independent labels and use actual
  explanations or solutions as evidence; satisfaction alone cannot establish learning.

### 10. Practicing one response need not transfer to another

Pan & Rickard (2017), _Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied_,
[Does retrieval practice enhance learning and transfer relative to restudy?](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000124).
[Author full text](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_JEPA_2017.pdf).

- **Design/outcome:** UC San Diego undergraduates, four experiments (including
  3a/3b); analyzed samples 54, 59, 49/52 and 113. Term–definition practice with
  feedback; multiple-choice or short-answer tests after 48 hours.
- **Finding:** Benefits over no training were clear; compared with restudy,
  reversing the practiced term–definition direction yielded little transfer.
- **Confidence/limit:** Moderate-to-high for this bounded comparison; no claim that
  all retrieval practice lacks transfer.
- **App inference:** Review variants for the reasoning they demand. New numbers or
  wording alone do not establish transfer; assess explanation, application and
  discrimination when those are the approved goals.

## Preliminary AI-writing neuroscience: a claim boundary

Kosmyna et al., [Your Brain on ChatGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872), is listed
as an arXiv preprint, latest version 2025-12-31. It studied essay writing with
ChatGPT, search or no tools: 54 participants in the first three sessions, with 18
in the fourth-session switch. Outcomes included EEG connectivity, essay measures,
ownership and ability to quote one's recent essay. These are not course retention,
general intelligence or a diagnosis of cognitive decline.

Only metadata, abstract and the authors' limitations were checked here, not all
216 pages or a reanalysis. The [authors' project page](https://www.media.mit.edu/projects/your-brain-on-chatgpt/overview/)
acknowledges task/population limits, EEG localization limits and missing analysis
of writing subtasks; it explicitly rejects brain-damage language. A separate
[methodological comment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856), itself a preprint, raises
design, reproducibility and EEG-analysis concerns. Treat this as a research question,
not evidence that LecturePilot prevents or causes cognitive damage.

## Implications for the presentation freeze

These are design inferences, not interventions validated by the papers above:

1. **Keep the evidence boundary visible.** Supported work, a fresh unaided attempt
   and a later check are different observations. Never equate reading completion,
   confidence, speed, EEG or engagement with mastery.
2. **Give each representation a teaching job.** A source-backed worked example
   can connect a table, formula and figure through one explicitly stated change.
   Introduce prediction only when the learner has enough context to reason.
3. **Require an explanation after observation.** A changed display or correct
   click alone does not show the learner understood its relation to the concept.
4. **Audit transfer and help leakage.** Check that reviewed variants exercise the
   approved reasoning, remain private until issued, and cannot acquire independent
   status after help. An interesting teaching example must not expose a hidden task.
5. **Keep the final iteration small.** Wording, explanation structure, existing
   evidence transitions and one representative demo are suitable freeze candidates.
   A new VR platform, sensing layer or game economy needs its own evaluation.

## Remaining evidence gaps

No cited experiment tests LecturePilot, its LLM assessment, its reviewed language
variants or its adaptive sequence. Display-based factual recall is not equivalent
to mathematical transfer, exam performance or durable independent learning.
The reviewed neuroscience cannot determine the best visualization for every
concept, an exact delay policy, or a student's individual cognitive state.
An eventual evaluation should compare matched content and study opportunity,
then test fresh, unassisted application after a stated delay. Keep that study
separate from tomorrow's demonstration and its product-readiness checks.
